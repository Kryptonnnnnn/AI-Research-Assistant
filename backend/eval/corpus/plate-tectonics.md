# Plate Tectonics

The outer shell of the Earth, the lithosphere, is broken into about a dozen large plates and many smaller ones that move slowly over the softer asthenosphere beneath. Typical speeds are a few centimetres per year, about as fast as fingernails grow. The theory of plate tectonics explains the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain ranges.

## Plate boundaries

At divergent boundaries plates move apart and new crust forms as magma rises; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the classic example. At convergent boundaries plates collide. When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks beneath in a process called subduction, creating deep ocean trenches and chains of volcanoes such as the Andes. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily and the crust is crumpled upward into mountains like the Himalayas. At transform boundaries, plates slide past each other horizontally, as along the San Andreas Fault in California, and the stored strain is released in earthquakes.

## Evidence and driving forces

Several lines of evidence support the theory: the matching coastlines and fossils of South America and Africa, symmetric magnetic stripes on the sea floor on either side of mid-ocean ridges, and the global pattern of earthquakes along plate edges. The main driving forces are slab pull, the weight of a sinking plate dragging the rest of the plate behind it, and ridge push, along with slow convection in the mantle.
