# How mRNA Vaccines Work

Messenger RNA vaccines teach the body to recognise a pathogen without exposing it to the pathogen itself. Instead of injecting a weakened virus or a purified protein, the vaccine delivers a short strand of messenger RNA that carries instructions for making one harmless piece of the pathogen, typically a surface protein.

## Delivery

Naked RNA is fragile and is quickly destroyed by enzymes in the body. The strand is therefore wrapped in tiny lipid nanoparticles, droplets of fat that protect the RNA and help it enter cells after an injection into the upper arm muscle. Once inside, the cell's ribosomes read the instructions and build the protein. The messenger RNA is short-lived and is broken down within days; it never enters the cell nucleus and does not alter DNA.

## Immune response

The cell displays fragments of the newly made protein on its surface. Immune cells recognise these fragments as foreign and respond in two ways: B cells produce antibodies that can neutralise the pathogen, and T cells learn to find and destroy infected cells. Memory cells persist afterwards, so a later real infection triggers a faster and stronger response.

## Side effects and storage

Common short-term reactions include a sore arm, fatigue, headache and mild fever, which reflect the immune system doing its work and usually resolve within two days. Because the lipid particles and RNA degrade at room temperature, many early mRNA vaccines required ultra-cold freezers for storage, which complicated distribution to remote regions. Newer formulations aim to improve stability.
