# Inflation: A Short Primer

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general level of prices, which reduces the purchasing power of money. It is usually measured by the percentage change in a consumer price index, a weighted average of the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by typical households.

## Causes

Economists distinguish demand-pull inflation, where total spending grows faster than the economy can produce, from cost-push inflation, where higher input costs such as energy or wages are passed on to consumers. Expectations also matter: if households and firms expect prices to rise, they raise wages and prices in advance, which can make the expected inflation self-fulfilling.

## Policy responses

Central banks typically aim for low and stable inflation, often around two percent per year. Their main tool is the policy interest rate. Raising the rate makes borrowing more expensive, cools spending and investment, and eventually slows price growth, while lowering it stimulates the economy. Changes in interest rates affect prices with a delay of many months, which makes timing difficult.

## Costs of inflation

Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth from lenders to borrowers and erodes the real value of fixed incomes and savings. High and volatile inflation makes planning difficult and can discourage investment. Deflation, a fall in prices, is also harmful because it encourages households to postpone purchases and raises the real burden of debt.
