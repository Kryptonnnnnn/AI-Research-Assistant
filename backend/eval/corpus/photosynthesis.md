# Photosynthesis in Plants

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar molecules. It takes place mainly in the leaves, inside organelles called chloroplasts. The overall reaction consumes carbon dioxide and water and releases oxygen as a by-product.

## Light-dependent reactions

The first stage occurs in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. Chlorophyll pigments absorb red and blue wavelengths most strongly, while green light is mostly reflected, which is why leaves look green. The absorbed energy splits water molecules, releasing oxygen, protons and electrons. The electrons travel along a transport chain and drive the production of ATP and NADPH, two small molecules that carry energy and reducing power to the next stage.

## The Calvin cycle

The second stage takes place in the stroma, the fluid surrounding the thylakoids. In the Calvin cycle the enzyme RuBisCO attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar. Using ATP and NADPH from the first stage, the cycle reduces the resulting compounds and produces three-carbon sugars that the plant assembles into glucose, starch and cellulose. RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme on Earth, yet it is slow and sometimes grabs oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, a wasteful side reaction known as photorespiration.

## Factors that limit the rate

Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature all limit how fast a leaf can fix carbon. At low light, the rate rises almost linearly with brightness. At high light the rate plateaus because the enzymes become the bottleneck. Water stress causes stomata, the small pores on the leaf surface, to close, which prevents carbon dioxide from entering and sharply reduces photosynthesis. Some plants such as maize use a C4 pathway that concentrates carbon dioxide around RuBisCO and therefore performs better in hot, dry climates.
